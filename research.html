Research Projects

AI's growing demand for computing comes at a steep energy cost. Much of that cost is set not only by physics, but also by the boundaries in how chips are built and designed. Our mission is to break these boundaries. The first is the wall between memory and computation. We develop Compute-in-Memory (CIM) technologies, from analog and digital CIM circuits to the dataflows, mappings and pipelining schemes that turn them into complete AI systems. The second is the "abstraction tax" of layer-by-layer chip design. We build design automation that goes directly from a system specification to optimized circuits and learns from every design it produces. Both rest on one conviction: the greatest gains in efficiency lie between the layers, not within them. Together, they lead toward AI hardware that is faster, smaller and more energy-efficient, and much easier to create.

Compute-in-Memory (CIM) Circuit and Architecture

Modern AI workloads, from convolutional networks to large Transformer models, are increasingly limited by the energy cost of moving data rather than of computing with it. My research addresses this "memory wall" through a cross-layer co-design of energy-efficient, low-power AI hardware built on Compute-in-Memory (CIM), spanning circuits, architecture and power delivery. At the circuit level, I design CIM macros that perform multiply-accumulate operations directly inside the memory array. These include analog CIM, which uses current- or charge-domain computation for massive parallelism and high energy efficiency, and digital CIM, which offers bit-exact accuracy, flexible precision and robustness that scales with the process node. At the architecture level, I build dataflows, workload mappings and pipelining schemes around the strengths and limits of these macros. The goals are to maximize array utilization, and overlap computation across layers and within each macro so that efficiency at the macro level carries through to the whole system. Together, these layers form one co-optimized stack aimed at large gains in TOPS/W and throughput, enabling AI hardware that scales from edge devices to the datacenter.

Electronic Design Automation (EDA)

Modern chips are designed through layers of abstraction. A system specification is refined into an architecture, then a register-transfer description, then a gate-level netlist, and finally a layout built from a fixed library of standard cells. These layers are what make billion-transistor designs manageable. But each hand-off between them carries a cost: architectural templates chosen in advance, synthesis heuristics that optimize only locally, cell libraries fixed long before the circuit is known, and safety margins added at every step. Together, these losses form an abstraction tax: power, speed and area that the underlying technology could deliver but layer-by-layer design leaves on the table. Our research develops design automation that recovers this tax. Our tools search across layer boundaries, going directly from a system specification to circuits built at the standard-cell level, and learn from every design they have produced before. The goal is circuits that are faster, smaller and more energy-efficient than abstraction-based methods can achieve, with a lighter, more automated design process.
